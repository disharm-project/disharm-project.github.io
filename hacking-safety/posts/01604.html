This was published 2 years ago Add articles to your saved list and come back to them any time. “I never thought dobbing and snitching was part of the Australian character,” said an aggrieved Tony Abbott, photographed by a member of the public walking without a mask on Manly Beach, and copping a $500 fine last week. As far as he was concerned, it seemed, the dobber was not a brave citizen-enforcer with the public interest at heart, but just a busybody who probably didn’t like his politics and – if he cared about the absence of a mask at all – didn’t have the guts to confront him in person. And this weekend there were thousands like him on the beaches of Manly and Bondi. The moment at Fairy Bower that got Tony Abbott into trouble. The question of dobbing had been on quite a few people’s minds since NSW Police Minister David Elliott called on the public to dob in those who flout lockdown laws or expose others to COVID-19, announced on the front page of The Sydney Morning Herald on August 17 under the provocative heading: “Minister urges public to become informers.” “Isn’t it un-Australian to be a dobber?” asked Robert Ballinger, of Pymble, in as letter to the editor the next day. And you can understand why. Back in 1997, ANU linguist Anna Wierzbicka published a book called Understanding Cultures through their Key Words. Along with “bullshit” and “bastard”, among Australia’s key words were “dobbing in”, glossed by Wierzbicka as a negative affirmation and celebration of mateship, “condemning, with contempt, anyone who betrays it”. Contempt for dobbing was general in the West during the Cold War, though only Australians used the term. Encouragement to citizens to inform on their neighbours was seen as a despicable characteristic of totalitarian states, one of the hallmarks of George Orwell’s 1984. In the US, popular attitudes to dobbing in were always more ambiguous, and the Cold War intensified this. At the time of the Monica Lewinsky/President Bill Clinton scandal, when a form of dobbing known as “whistleblowing” was coming into favour, Lewinsky’s friend Linda Tripp introduced a new wrinkle by dobbing her in. Then came 9/11, ushering in a 21st century in which it was a civic duty to participate in the fight against terrorists. I lived in the US then, and remember driving down a New Jersey Turnpike newly adorned with flashing messages overhead instructing citizens to “Report any suspicious persons”. If the US leads, Australia cannot be far behind, and so it was with dobbing in. By 2014, according to Macquarie Dictionary editor Susan Butler in The Haitch Factor, the old Australian pejorative was being used in a positive sense (“dob in a dumpster” to your local council). Still, Elliott got enough conservative blowback to modify his message. When the second weekend of anti-lockdown protests loomed, he struck a different note, warning protesters that “going by last Saturday’s rally, you’ve got probably five of your fellow citizens that are prepared to dob you in”. “Now,” he added, “I don’t like that. That’s awful, it’s counterintuitive to everything that I believe Australians stand for.” But Elliott didn’t say not to do it. Contradictory? Yes. Ethically ambiguous? Yes. And yet this may be one of the ambiguities that in the age of COVID we have to live with. Sheila Fitzpatrick is a professor of history at the Australian Catholic University and an honorary professor at the University of Sydney.
